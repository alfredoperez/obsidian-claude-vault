# Surface: Explainer

Editorial hubs, digests and summaries meant to be scanned, audio scripts and narration, video
scripts, and any document whose reader may not know the vocabulary yet.

Load `core-voice.md` first. Audio delivery mechanics (pacing, pronunciation, voice tags) are out of scope here; this profile owns how the
explanation itself is built.

**Provenance:** this register is modeled on the AI Daily Brief podcast's explanatory style
(Nathaniel Whittemore), at the user's explicit request after reviewing two full transcripts. The
complaint it fixes: abstract titles, expert vocabulary before definitions, calls to action before
the reader knows what the thing is, and too many terms per sentence.

## The Governing Rule: Nobody Needs the Vocabulary to Follow You

Write for a smart reader who does not know the words yet. Every term earns its place by being
defined the moment it appears, and every claim arrives after the reader has seen the plain facts
that make it land.

## Rules

1. **Lead with the plain phenomenon, not the conclusion.** Open with the concrete observable fact,
   in words anyone knows, and analyze it later. The model opens "Everyone, it appears, hates data
   centers. Polls show the majority of Americans, both Democrats and Republicans, are opposed" and
   only then asks why. No abstract titles, no thesis-first openings.

2. **Ask the question out loud before answering it.** Sections are questions a person would
   actually ask, then answers. "So why are people so angry about data centers? The short answer is
   it's everything."

3. **Define every term at first use, in one clause, before relying on it.** "A data center is
   basically a warehouse-sized computer that is plugged into industrial systems." Do this even for
   things half the audience knows, because the other half does not. Never stack a second new term
   on a sentence that just introduced one.

4. **Numbers carry their meaning with them.** "Opposition reached 75%, and strong support shrank
   from 9% to 4%": the movement and its significance travel with the figure. Never a bare number
   the reader has to interpret alone.

5. **Attribute positions to named people, quote briefly, then interpret.** "Investor Nick Carter
   wrote ... They are now learning that the internet requires physical infrastructure, and they
   are upset about it." Quote, then say what it means in your own words.

6. **Signpost transitions plainly.** "So starting off", "Now at this point, it's worth asking",
   "So where do we go from here?" The reader always knows where they are in the argument.

7. **End each unit with what it means for the reader.** A stance or a consequence, not a summary
   of what was just said. This includes the last section: no recap endings.

8. **Vary sentence length; talk like a person.** Long explanatory sentences are fine when each
   carries one idea. No machine-gun short sentences, no catchphrases. This matches the standing
   audio guidance.

9. **Steelman before dismissing.** Lay out the opposing position's actual concerns fairly, in
   their strongest form, before disagreeing with parts of them.

## Ordering

The register implies an order for any unit, from a section to a whole hub:

1. The plain phenomenon, in everyday words
2. The question it raises, asked out loud
3. The answer, with terms defined as they arrive and numbers carrying their meaning
4. Who says what, quoted briefly and then interpreted
5. What it means for the reader

Calls to action, links to go deeper, and jargon-dense detail all come after the reader knows what
the thing is, never before.

## Checklist

- [ ] The opening states a concrete observable fact in words anyone knows
- [ ] Every section heading or opening is a question a person would actually ask
- [ ] Every term is defined in one clause at first use; no sentence introduces two new terms
- [ ] Every number travels with its movement or significance
- [ ] Positions are attributed to named people, quoted briefly, then interpreted
- [ ] Transitions are plainly signposted
- [ ] Each unit ends with a stance or consequence, not a recap
- [ ] Sentence lengths vary; no catchphrases, no machine-gun bursts
- [ ] Opposing views are steelmanned before being dismissed
- [ ] No call to action appears before the reader knows what the thing is
